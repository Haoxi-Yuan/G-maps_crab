## 5.5 Factor Frequency and Contribution Analysis

Building on the structured factor extraction pipeline described in Section 4.5, we analyzed 106,575 pairwise comparison records across three bikeability dimensions (accessibility, comfort, and safety), achieving a 94.2% successful parsing rate. The extraction yielded 508,092 factor-level observations after normalization and deduplication, with an average of 5.1 factors extracted per comparison record. This section first characterizes the salience and influence of individual factors and their directionality across dimensions (Section 5.5.1), then examines systematic discrepancies between salience and influence to identify latent perceptual determinants and their planning implications (Section 5.5.2).

Across all three dimensions, the contribution hierarchy follows a consistent pattern: a small number of factors exert disproportionately large effects while the majority contribute more modestly. In accessibility, accessibility quality (OR = 1510) and POI density (OR = 664) dominate, reflecting the fundamental role of reachable destinations and service quality in shaping accessibility perceptions. Comfort is led by psychological comfort (OR = 2898), followed by building enclosure (OR = 398) and shade coverage (OR = 208)—a hierarchy in which the integrative experiential judgment outweighs any single physical feature. Safety is anchored by visibility (OR = 1617), consistent with research showing that sightline quality is the primary determinant of perceived traffic risk because it enables cyclists to anticipate and avoid conflicts (Ferenchak et al., 2025).

This contribution hierarchy does not align with attention patterns. Safety reasoning is tightly concentrated on a few dominant cues: the top three factors (visibility, conflict risk, mixed traffic) all exceed 80% mention rates, while the median safety factor falls below 15%. The accessibility dimension, by contrast, distributes attention more broadly across destination diversity, network structure, and spatial configuration, consistent with the multifaceted nature of accessibility perception. Comfort falls between these extremes, anchored by two near-universal perceptual cues—building enclosure (97.6%) and green view rate (96.4%)—that appear in virtually all comparisons regardless of their relative influence ranking. The divergence between attention and contribution patterns is further explored in Section 5.5.2.

The directionality results confirm that most factors operate as positive contributors: features whose presence or enhancement improves perceived bikeability. Notable exceptions are risk-related factors in the safety dimension—conflict risk, mixed traffic, and traffic volume—where higher levels degrade cycling conditions and should be targeted for reduction. Among the positive contributors, street width merits particular attention for its dimension-dependent behavior. Wider streets contribute positively to accessibility (β = 3.70) by facilitating route options and spatial legibility, yet contribute only marginally to safety (β = 0.90) and do not appear among the significant comfort factors. This pattern is consistent with the well-documented tension in urban design between vehicular capacity and pedestrian-scale spatial quality: wider streets may enhance connectivity and sightlines but erode the sense of enclosure and human scale that cyclists find comfortable (Ewing & Handy, 2009; Gehl, 2010). The most striking directional anomalies, however, involve factors that show *reversed* polarity across dimensions—infrastructure features conventionally expected to benefit cycling that instead exhibit negative effects on comfort or safety. These cross-dimensional reversals are discussed in Section 5.5.2.

### 5.5.2 Perception Gaps and Planning Implications

Comparing factor salience with factor influence reveals systematic discrepancies that illuminate the structure of human environmental perception and carry direct planning implications. Figure Z synthesizes this comparison by plotting each factor's salience percentile against its influence percentile. Points above the rank-equality diagonal represent factors whose influence exceeds their salience—latent yet powerful determinants of perceived bikeability. Points below the diagonal indicate factors that are readily perceptible and consistently recognized but weakly decisive.

**Latent but important factors.** The most striking salience–influence gaps appear among localized infrastructure features in the accessibility dimension. Accessibility facility (mentioned in only 8.4% of comparisons) ranks 3rd in influence, yielding an odds ratio of 105—meaning a streetscape with better transit stops or service access points is over 100 times more likely to win when this factor is recognized. Access points (12.1% mention rate, OR = 57) and street interface continuity (8.2%, OR = 26) show comparable patterns: rarely foregrounded in reasoning, yet strongly decisive when they are. These features are not prominent visual elements in the streetscape; they describe fine-grained connectivity and interface qualities that operate below the threshold of routine environmental awareness. In the comfort dimension, shade coverage (29.2%, OR = 208) and tree shade corridor (6.2%, OR = 24) are mentioned far less often than building enclosure or green view rate but rank among the top five contributors. Thermal comfort features, it appears, serve as powerful differentiators in streetscape preference—when one environment offers meaningfully better shade, this advantage strongly shapes the outcome, even though shade is not a primary focus of conscious attention.

This pattern aligns with theories of environmental cognition suggesting that perception of place quality involves both explicitly attended features and implicitly processed contextual cues. Kaplan (1995) distinguished between "informational" features—elements that contribute to spatial coherence and legibility—and "salient" features that command immediate attention, noting that the former often exert disproportionate influence on preference despite being difficult to articulate. The latent factors identified here—access infrastructure, shade continuity, street interface quality—correspond closely to this informational category. Their low salience but high influence suggests that they contribute to what cyclists experience as a holistic sense of "a good street for cycling" without being consciously catalogued as discrete environmental attributes.

**Readily perceptible but weakly decisive factors.** The opposite pattern characterizes several visually conspicuous or experientially salient features. In the safety dimension, obstacles (13.2% mention rate) yield a near-zero, non-significant coefficient: while people notice and discuss obstructions, their presence rarely tips the balance between streetscapes. Traffic volume (10.5%) shows a similar pattern. More revealingly, among significant safety factors, mixed traffic conditions (80.4% mention rate, β = 3.54) produce an influence coefficient substantially lower than visibility (95.2%, β = 7.39) despite comparable salience—a nearly twofold gap in decisiveness for factors of similar perceptual prominence. In comfort, cycling experience (20.5%, β = 0.96) functions as a broad evaluative descriptor that adds narrative context to the reasoning without sharply discriminating between alternatives.

The limited decisiveness of these factors likely reflects restricted variance rather than irrelevance. Most central Shanghai streetscapes present comparable levels of sidewalk encroachment and parked vehicles (Section 5.1), so obstacles provide little discriminating power between alternatives. This interpretation aligns with Stamps (2004), who demonstrated that features commanding visual attention do not necessarily predict overall environmental preference—a pattern our factor-level analysis corroborates at the streetscape scale.

**Cross-dimensional anomalies: when infrastructure presence reduces perceived quality.** Perhaps the most conceptually revealing finding involves factors that show reversed polarity when evaluated outside their primary domain. Table 5 marks these with "(−)": cycle path exhibits negative coefficients in both comfort (β = −2.30) and safety (β = −1.60), and safety feeling shows a negative effect on comfort (β = −2.15). These reversals indicate that the presence of cycling infrastructure—conventionally assumed to benefit all aspects of bikeability—can be associated with *worse* perceived comfort and safety under certain conditions.

The most plausible interpretation involves a confounding mechanism rooted in urban morphology. In Shanghai, dedicated cycle paths are disproportionately located along major arterial roads characterized by high traffic volumes, wide carriageways, and limited green buffer zones. When the model controls for the dominant comfort factors (building enclosure, green view rate, psychological comfort), the residual association between cycle path presence and comfort becomes negative—revealing that streets with cycling infrastructure tend to be the very streets whose spatial and environmental qualities diminish comfort. The infrastructure itself does not cause discomfort; rather, it serves as a proxy for the traffic-oriented road typology in which it is typically embedded. A parallel logic applies in the safety dimension: cycle paths along busy arterials may paradoxically reduce perceived safety because the surrounding traffic environment overwhelms the protective signal of the infrastructure itself.

This finding carries a significant implication: **the presence of cycling infrastructure is not equivalent to perceived cycling quality**. Bike lanes and cycle paths are necessary but insufficient conditions for bikeability. Their effectiveness depends critically on the spatial and environmental context in which they are embedded—a principle that aligns with growing evidence from cycling behavior research showing that infrastructure utilization rates depend heavily on surrounding land use, traffic conditions, and streetscape quality rather than infrastructure provision alone (Buehler & Dill, 2016; Winters et al., 2011).

**Planning implications.** These salience–influence discrepancies translate into actionable guidance through three channels.

Latent but important factors represent high-leverage intervention targets that are systematically undervalued in conventional assessment. Improving access point design, extending continuous tree shade corridors, and enhancing street interface continuity may yield disproportionate returns in perceived bikeability relative to their cost and visibility. These interventions align with emerging evidence on "invisible infrastructure"—the network of small-scale design features that cumulatively shape route attractiveness without being individually prominent (Forsyth & Krizek, 2011). Their low salience means they are easily overlooked in participatory planning exercises, where respondents tend to foreground visually dominant features such as greenery or traffic conditions (Krenn et al., 2015). The contribution analysis provides a corrective lens, revealing where actual influence diverges from expressed attention.

Readily perceptible but weakly decisive factors, conversely, warrant cautious resource allocation. The frequent mention of obstacles or traffic volume in environmental assessments might lead planners to prioritize obstacle removal or traffic calming as primary interventions. The contribution analysis suggests, however, that their marginal contribution to perceived bikeability is limited relative to the latent factors identified above. This does not imply that removing severe obstacles is unimportant in absolute terms, but rather that planning resources may achieve greater perceptual returns when directed toward shade infrastructure, access connectivity, and visibility enhancement.

The cross-dimensional anomalies add a further layer of nuance. Cycling infrastructure planning cannot be treated as a single-objective optimization problem: interventions that enhance accessibility may inadvertently erode comfort if the spatial context is not addressed simultaneously. Street widening, for example, may improve accessibility and safety sightlines but diminish the enclosure and human-scale qualities that contribute to cycling comfort. The negative association between cycle path presence and perceived quality in the comfort and safety dimensions reinforces the case for integrated design strategies—combining infrastructure provision with contextual improvements in greenery, shade, and spatial enclosure—rather than treating bike lane installation as a standalone intervention (Gehl, 2010; Pucher & Buehler, 2017).
